# Atlas Training Model

> **Status:** Foundational — the permanent philosophy of how Atlas turns priorities into training.
> **Nature:** A *design philosophy for training*. It contains no software, APIs, schemas, prompts, pseudocode, algorithms, or specific exercises — by design. It defines how training is reasoned about, never which drill to run.
> **Position in the Intelligence layer:** [`coaching_dna.md`](coaching_dna.md) is the conscience. [`table_tennis_knowledge.md`](table_tennis_knowledge.md) is the material. [`atlas_reasoning_model.md`](atlas_reasoning_model.md) is the method. [`priority_engine.md`](priority_engine.md) decides what deserves attention. **This document turns that attention into training that actually produces learning.**
> **Precedence:** Coaching DNA governs. This document operationalizes Coaching DNA §11 (teaching and learning), §12 (adaptation), §13 (evaluation), §14 (long-term athlete development), and §15 (risk management), and applies the Knowledge Model's Training Methodology and Learning Progressions. It may not weaken any Atlas DNA principle.
> **Amendment:** deliberate and explicit, never incidental.

---

## Purpose

The Priority Engine ends with a small, honest set of priorities: a few things that deserve attention now, each with its reasoning, evidence, confidence, and stated deferrals. That is still not training. Knowing that a player's footwork limits everything else does not tell a coach what Tuesday should look like.

**The Training Model answers:**

> **"Once Atlas knows what is most important, how should training be designed?"**

It is the final bridge in the Intelligence layer — from *what matters* to *what actually happens on the table*. Its concern is the design logic of training: how a priority becomes an objective, how objectives sequence into progression, how progression adapts to reality, how training is evaluated, and when a priority is finished.

**What this document does not do.** It prescribes no drills, exercises, or routines. Specific practice belongs to the coach, who knows their players, their hall, their equipment, and their time. Atlas supplies the reasoning that makes training purposeful; the coach supplies the craft that makes it real. A model that dictated exercises would replace the coach's expertise rather than amplify it.

## What This Document Inherits

- **From the Priority Engine** — WORK NOW priorities are the input. MONITOR and WAIT items are *not* trained; they are watched or held. Training never quietly re-introduces what prioritization deliberately deferred.
- **From the Knowledge Model** — training methodology (closed to open practice, repetition against variability, feedback timing, load management, periodization, transfer) and learning progressions (prerequisite chains, the stages of skill acquisition, the movement from consistency to variability to pressure-tolerance).
- **From Coaching DNA** — meet the player where they are; build on strengths; sequence deliberately; allow productive struggle; reinforce over time; teach understanding, not imitation; aim for transfer. Plus adaptation, honest evaluation, the long arc, and safety as a precondition.
- **From the Decision Hierarchy** — Atlas DNA → Club DNA → Coach DNA → **Group DNA** → Player Context → Current Session. Training design flows through it without exception.
- **From the Product Principles** — training must be usable by a real coach under real time pressure, not a theoretically perfect plan nobody can run.

## Part I — Training Philosophy

### What Atlas Maximizes

- **Long-term development.** Training is designed for the multi-year athlete, not the next session's appearance of progress.
- **Meaningful learning.** The measure of a session is what durably changed, not what was covered.
- **Transfer to competition.** Skill that only exists in a drill has not been learned. Practice earns its value by surviving into real, pressured play.
- **Coach flexibility.** Training proposals leave room for the coach's judgment, methods, and improvisation in the moment.
- **Player engagement.** A player invested in the work learns faster and persists longer than one merely complying.
- **Consistency.** Steady, repeated, purposeful work produces development; sporadic intensity does not.
- **Adaptation.** The plan serves the player. When reality contradicts the plan, the plan yields.

### What Atlas Minimizes

- **Overtraining.** Load that exceeds capacity harms the athlete and degrades learning. More is not better; appropriate is better.
- **Unnecessary complexity.** A session a coach cannot run cleanly, or a player cannot understand, has already failed.
- **Premature specialization.** Narrowing a player too early trades a broad, durable foundation for a short-term edge.
- **Training without purpose.** Every element of training must trace to an objective, and every objective to a priority. Activity is not development.

### Core Beliefs

- **Training serves the priority, not the calendar.** Sessions exist to advance something specific, not to fill time.
- **Fewer objectives, pursued properly.** Concentration produces change; a session chasing everything achieves nothing.
- **Learning requires struggle.** Difficulty at the edge of ability is the mechanism, not a flaw to be smoothed away.
- **Consolidation is development.** Time spent making a fragile skill reliable is not a delay in progress; it *is* progress.
- **The coach runs the session.** Atlas proposes design and reasoning; the coach owns delivery, timing, tone, and every in-session decision.

## Part II — Training Objectives

An **objective** is what a period of training is meant to change. It is the translation of a priority into something trainable.

A legitimate training objective is:

- **Traceable.** It descends from a WORK NOW priority and carries that priority's reasoning with it. An objective nobody can trace to a priority should not exist.
- **Specific enough to design around.** Broad aspirations cannot shape practice; the objective must name what should change.
- **Bounded.** It has a scope and a rough horizon, so progress can be judged and completion recognized.
- **Achievable in sequence.** Its prerequisites are already in place, or the prerequisite becomes the objective instead.
- **Observable in its effects.** There must be some way to tell whether it is working, or evaluation becomes opinion.
- **Owned by the player's understanding.** The player should know what they are working on and why. Hidden objectives produce compliance, not learning.

**Session objectives** are narrower still: what *this* session contributes to the standing objective. A session rarely completes an objective; it advances it. Most sessions should carry one primary objective and, at most, a small amount of secondary work — usually consolidation of something previously trained.

## Part III — Training Constraints

Constraints bind design; they are not preferences to be traded against expected benefit.

**T1 — Safety is a precondition.** No training objective outranks the athlete's physical and psychological safety. Under uncertainty about harm, Atlas designs conservatively.

**T2 — Injury and fatigue reshape everything.** Training works *around* injury and recovery, never through them. Under fatigue, load reduces. This is not a compromise of training; it is training done correctly.

**T3 — Prerequisites cannot be skipped.** Practice that violates the natural order of development is inefficient at best and harmful at worst. If the foundation is absent, the foundation is the objective.

**T4 — The Decision Hierarchy governs.** Club philosophy, coach approach, group context, and player individuality each constrain design in that order. Training that contradicts a higher level is invalid.

**T5 — The coach's authority is final.** Atlas proposes; the coach disposes. Training design is never presented as a prescription the coach must execute.

**T6 — Realism.** A proposal must fit the actual session: its duration, the number of players, the coach's capacity to observe and feed, and the equipment available. An unrunnable plan is not a plan.

**T7 — Purpose.** Every element traces to an objective. Nothing is included because it is traditional, impressive, or fills time.

## Part IV — From Priority to Training

Training design proceeds in a fixed order. Each step constrains the next.

1. **Take the priority.** Only WORK NOW items enter. The priority's reasoning, evidence, and risks travel with it.
2. **Confirm readiness.** Check training readiness (below). If the player is not ready, the answer is not a modified session — it is back to prioritization.
3. **Translate into an objective.** Convert "this matters" into "this should change," bounded and traceable.
4. **Locate it on the progression.** Establish where the player currently sits in the skill's development, and what the next legitimate step is. Design begins from the player's actual position, never from an ideal.
5. **Choose the training character.** Determine what *kind* of practice serves this stage — more closed and repetitive for early acquisition, more open and variable as reliability grows. This is a decision about character, not about specific exercises.
6. **Set the context.** Individual, group, or hybrid; where it sits in the microcycle; how it fits the session at hand.
7. **Apply load and recovery judgment.** Fit the work to current capacity, and treat recovery as part of the design.
8. **Define progress indicators and a re-evaluation moment.** Decide in advance what would show this is working and when it will be reconsidered. Without this, evaluation later becomes retrospective storytelling.
9. **Hand to the coach with reasoning attached.** The proposal explains itself and leaves room for the coach's craft.

## Part V — Learning, Progression, and Readiness

### Skill Acquisition

Skill develops through recognizable stages: an early stage focused on understanding and rough execution, a refining stage where reliability grows, and a later stage where execution becomes largely automatic and available under pressure. **Training must match the stage.** Demanding pressure-tolerance from a skill still in early acquisition produces frustration and reinforces error; drilling a nearly automatic skill in closed conditions wastes time that transfer work would repay.

Atlas designs to the player's *actual* stage, which is determined by evidence rather than by how long they have been practising.

### Learning Progression

Progression follows the natural order the Knowledge Model describes: prerequisites before dependents, and — within a skill — from **consistency**, to **variability**, to **pressure-tolerance**. Consistency without variability produces a skill that collapses when conditions change. Variability without consistency produces unreliable improvisation. Pressure-tolerance attempted before both is simply failure under load.

Progression is advanced when evidence supports it, not on a schedule. Moving on because time has passed is as harmful as never moving on at all.

### Training Progression

Progression across sessions has direction. Each block of training builds on the last, revisits what needs consolidation, and extends toward the next step. Two failures are equally common and equally damaging: **progressing too fast**, which builds on an unstable base, and **progressing too slowly**, which wastes development time and erodes engagement. Atlas watches for both.

### Training Readiness

Readiness asks whether this player can productively absorb this work *now*. It considers physical capacity, prerequisite skill, psychological state, fatigue, injury status, motivation, and recent load. Readiness is not willingness — a motivated player may still be unready, and readiness may exist where enthusiasm does not.

**Where readiness is absent, the correct response is to change the objective or the load — not to proceed and hope.**

### Transfer to Competition

Transfer is the point of training and the primary test of its value. Work that improves drill performance without improving play has failed, however clean it looks.

Transfer is pursued by moving deliberately from closed toward open and match-like conditions as reliability grows, by introducing genuine variability and decision-making rather than only repetition, and by exposing skills to pressure before competition demands it. Atlas treats **evidence of transfer** — improvement visible in real play — as the strongest indicator that an objective is succeeding, and drill-only improvement as weak evidence.

### Training Variety and Training Consistency

These are complements, not opposites, and both are required.

- **Consistency** provides the repeated, purposeful contact with an objective that consolidation requires. Constantly changing focus prevents anything from taking root.
- **Variety** provides the varied conditions under which a skill becomes robust and transferable, and sustains engagement over long periods.

The resolution: **consistent objectives, varied practice.** The *what* stays stable long enough to produce change; the *how* varies to build durability and maintain engagement. Variety that changes the objective every session is not variety — it is drift.

## Part VI — Load, Recovery, and Fatigue

### Training Load

Load is the total demand training places on the athlete — physical, cognitive, and emotional. Cognitive and emotional load are real and frequently underestimated: a session demanding intense concentration or confronting a frustrating weakness carries a cost even when physically light.

Load is designed to fit current capacity and to accumulate sustainably. **Sustainability outranks intensity**: growth that cannot be maintained is not growth.

### Recovery

Recovery is part of training, not an absence of it. Adaptation happens during recovery, not during effort; training without adequate recovery produces fatigue rather than development. Rest is a legitimate and sometimes correct training decision, and Atlas proposes it without apology.

### Fatigue Management

Fatigue lowers the ceiling on everything — learning slows, execution degrades, injury risk rises. Under fatigue, Atlas reduces load and lowers technical demand rather than pushing through. Fatigue is read as information about capacity, never as weakness.

**Under fatigue and uncertainty, the safe default is less.** A session reduced for fatigue has not failed; it has adapted correctly.

## Part VII — Individual, Group, and Hybrid Training

This is the section where training design meets organizational reality. Not every session is individual, and Atlas must be excellent at group training rather than treating it as a compromise.

### The Foundational Facts

- **Groups are defined by the coach and the club**, with whatever names and purposes they choose. **Atlas never imposes a fixed group taxonomy** and never assumes what a group's name means. It learns each club's actual organization.
- **Players may belong to group training, individual training, or both.** Which context applies is determined by the session being designed.
- **Group DNA supplies the group's philosophy, objectives, developmental context, and training characteristics** — it is a genuine layer of the hierarchy, not a scheduling detail.

### Individual Training

The simplest case: one player, whose WORK NOW priorities directly become the session's objectives, adapted to their readiness and the coach's approach. Individual training offers maximum precision and maximum coach attention, and is therefore the natural home for priorities that are specific, delicate, or divergent from the group.

### Group Training — Coherence Without Uniformity

The central challenge: **how can one session satisfy club objectives, group objectives, and individual priorities without losing coherence?**

The answer is not to run several sessions simultaneously, which fragments the coach's attention and serves nobody well. Nor is it to average the group into a single generic session that fits no one. The resolution is **a shared spine with individual differentiation inside it.**

**1. Find genuine convergence.** Players grouped by a club usually share a level, a stage, and often the same underlying limiters — the Knowledge Model's root-cause reasoning frequently reduces several apparent individual problems to one shared foundation. The first task is to find the developmental theme that *honestly* serves most of the group. Convergence must be discovered, not manufactured; forcing a shared theme that fits nobody is worse than admitting divergence.

**2. Build the session on a shared spine.** The group works within one coherent activity, with one dominant purpose. This preserves the coach's ability to observe, feed, correct, and manage the session as a whole.

**3. Differentiate within the shared activity.** The same activity carries different individual emphases: what each player is asked to attend to, what constraint is placed on them, what counts as success for them. The group does one thing; each player pursues their own priority inside it. This is how one session serves many priorities without fragmenting.

**4. Surface genuine divergence honestly.** Where a player's priority cannot be served inside the shared spine, Atlas says so plainly rather than averaging it away. That priority becomes individual work, is addressed in a differentiated portion of the session, or is deferred with its reason stated. **Divergence is never disguised as coherence.**

**5. Never sacrifice individual safety.** Group coherence shapes what is trained together; it never overrides an individual's protection. A player who is injured, fatigued, or unready is adapted for individually within the group session — always.

**6. Respect the group's own identity.** Group DNA carries the group's purpose and developmental context. A group with a developmental purpose and one with a competitive purpose warrant genuinely different sessions from identical individual evidence.

**Group training done well is not a dilution of individual coaching.** A well-chosen shared theme often serves each player better than an isolated session would, because it supplies varied opposition, real decision-making, competitive context, and the motivational pull of training alongside others.

### Hybrid Training

Where a player trains both individually and in a group, the two threads must be **coherent, complementary, and jointly accounted**.

- **Coherent.** Both serve the same standing priorities. Individual and group work pulling in different directions confuses the player and wastes both.
- **Complementary.** Each does what it does best: individual work for precision, delicate correction, and divergent needs; group work for variability, opposition, pressure, and transfer.
- **Jointly accounted.** Load, fatigue, and recovery are considered **across both contexts together**. The most common hybrid failure is treating the two as separate ledgers and unknowingly overloading the athlete.

## Part VIII — Identity Integration and Coach Flexibility

### Club Philosophy Integration

The club's orientation and aims shape the character of training under its roof. Two clubs may design legitimately different training from identical evidence, and Atlas serves each rather than imposing one house style — constrained only by Atlas DNA.

### Group Identity Integration

Group DNA determines the shared purpose, developmental context, and training characteristics of a group session. It is the layer that makes a group session coherent as *this group's* session rather than a generic one.

### Player Identity Integration

Player DNA personalizes everything within the levels above: age, stage, goals, learning speed, temperament, physical condition, strengths, preferences, and health. Two players with the same objective may need different framing, different progression speed, and different amounts of challenge. **The same objective is not the same training.**

### Coach Flexibility

Training proposals are **designed to be modified**. Atlas provides objectives, reasoning, sequencing, and constraints; the coach provides the exercises, the timing, the language, and every in-session judgment.

Atlas explicitly leaves room for: the coach's preferred methods and exercise vocabulary; in-session improvisation as things unfold; the coach's read of mood, energy, and teachable moments; and outright rejection of a proposal. A coach's modification is not a defect in the plan — it is the plan working as intended, and it is signal that refines Coach DNA.

## Part IX — Microcycle Thinking and Competition Preparation

### Microcycle Thinking

A session is not an island. Sessions within a short repeating cycle must relate to each other deliberately:

- **Alternate demand.** Heavy and light, high-cognitive and consolidating, so load distributes and recovery is possible.
- **Space repetition.** Skills consolidate through spaced, repeated contact, not through single saturated sessions.
- **Sequence within the cycle.** Work requiring freshness and precision belongs where the athlete is fresh; consolidation tolerates fatigue better than new learning does.
- **Preserve continuity.** Objectives persist across the cycle. A cycle that changes focus every session cannot produce consolidation.

Atlas thinks in cycles rather than isolated sessions, and in seasons rather than isolated cycles — while leaving the concrete scheduling to the coach, who owns the calendar.

### Competition Preparation

As competition approaches, training shifts from **building** toward **stabilizing, sharpening, and preparing**. Disruptive technical change is deferred — destabilizing a skill shortly before competition usually costs more than it gains. Emphasis moves toward what is already reliable, toward tactical readiness, toward pressure exposure, and toward confidence. Load is managed so the athlete arrives fresh.

Foundational work deferred for a competition is **held with the competition named as its condition**, so it resumes afterward rather than being lost. **Safety is never deferred for a competition.** After competition, training returns to development, informed by what the competition revealed — often the most valuable evidence available.

## Part X — Evaluation, Regression, Plateau, and Completion

### Training Evaluation

Evaluating *the training* asks whether the design is working: is it producing change, is it appropriate to readiness, is it being delivered as intended, and is the load sustainable? Training that is not producing change is a design problem to diagnose, not an effort problem to push harder against.

### Progress Evaluation

Evaluating *the player* measures against their own trajectory, over a horizon long enough to distinguish trend from fluctuation, across the whole athlete rather than one dimension. **Transfer into real play is the strongest evidence of progress**; drill-only improvement is weak evidence and must be labeled as such. Progress is reported honestly in both directions — real gains named clearly, stalled progress not disguised.

### Plateau Management

A plateau is a sustained absence of progress despite appropriate work. It is a signal to re-examine, never to simply intensify.

Atlas considers: whether the diagnosis was wrong (a symptom trained instead of a root cause); whether a prerequisite is missing; whether the training character no longer suits the stage (commonly, closed practice continuing past its usefulness); whether load or fatigue is suppressing learning; whether the block is motivational rather than technical; and whether the plateau is in fact consolidation, which can look like stagnation while being exactly what the skill needs.

**Plateaus frequently mean the objective is wrong, not that the player is failing.** The usual response is to return the question to prioritization rather than to push harder.

### Regression Handling

Regression — a skill getting worse — is treated first as information, not alarm. Common and legitimate causes include: fatigue or accumulated load; the normal, temporary disruption that follows a deliberate technical change; pressure exposing what closed practice concealed; injury or physical change; and psychological factors.

Atlas distinguishes **true regression** from **noise** and from **expected temporary disruption** before acting. A skill mid-rebuild is *supposed* to get worse before it gets better; treating that as failure and reversing course wastes the investment. Where regression is real and sustained, the objective and its approach are re-examined. Where regression involves pain or injury risk, it is escalated immediately.

### Training Completion Criteria

An objective is complete when the skill is **reliable under realistic conditions and transfers into real play** — not when it has been performed correctly in a drill, and not when a planned period has elapsed.

Completion is a transition, not a disappearance: a completed objective typically moves to **monitoring** for consolidation and durability rather than vanishing. Declaring completion too early is one of the most common training failures, because a skill that works in practice and collapses under pressure was never learned.

### Reevaluation

Training is reconsidered: immediately on any safety, injury, or health signal; when readiness materially changes; when progress indicators show the objective advancing, plateauing, or regressing; when the coach modifies or rejects the design; when competition or season context shifts; when group composition or purpose changes; when the underlying priority itself changes; and periodically, so no objective persists unexamined.

**Reevaluation returns to prioritization when appropriate.** If the priority is no longer right, the correct fix is a better priority, not better training of the wrong thing.

## Part XI — The Shape of a Training Recommendation

Conceptually, every training recommendation carries the following. This describes what makes a recommendation *legitimate* — not a format or an implementation.

- **Training Objective** — what this training is meant to change.
- **Why This Objective** — the reasoning, traced back to the WORK NOW priority and its evidence.
- **Target Player(s)** — who this is for, individually identified even within a group.
- **Target Group(s)** — the group context this belongs to, where applicable, and how the objective fits that group's identity.
- **Expected Development** — what should improve, and what evidence of transfer would demonstrate it.
- **Estimated Time Horizon** — roughly how long before meaningful change should be expected, stated honestly as a range rather than with false precision.
- **Training Constraints** — safety, injury, fatigue, load, prerequisites, and practical session limits that bound the design.
- **Progress Indicators** — what would show this is working, decided in advance rather than reconstructed afterward.
- **Reevaluation Moment** — the condition or point at which this should be reconsidered.

A recommendation missing its reasoning, its constraints, or its progress indicators is incomplete and must not be presented as settled.

## Open Architectural Questions

Genuine unresolved tensions, surfaced rather than silently resolved.

1. **The session attention budget remains unquantified (High).** This document explains *how* one session serves several priorities — a shared spine with differentiation — but not *how many* individual priorities a single group session can carry before the coach's attention fragments and differentiation becomes theatre. The limit is real and depends on group size, coach capacity, and player independence. This partially answers the Priority Engine's open question on reconciling per-player priorities into one session, but does not close it.

2. **Ownership of the calendar horizon is undrawn (High).** This document establishes microcycle thinking but deliberately leaves concrete scheduling to the coach. Whether a future **Session Planner** owns cycle construction, and where its responsibility begins and this model's ends, is not yet defined. Without that boundary the two will duplicate or contradict each other.

3. **Completion, plateau, and consolidation are hard to distinguish in practice (High).** All three can present as "no visible change." The document names the distinction as essential and gives the questions to ask, but there is no reliable discriminator. Mistaking consolidation for a plateau causes premature abandonment; mistaking a plateau for consolidation wastes months.

4. **Load accounting across hybrid contexts is asserted, not solved (Medium/High).** The requirement to account load jointly across group and individual training is clear, but how load from different contexts, coaches, or even clubs is combined into one honest picture of an athlete's total demand is unresolved — and matters most precisely where overtraining risk is highest.

5. **Evidence of transfer is the standard but not the specification (Medium).** Transfer is named as the primary measure of training value and the strongest completion criterion, yet what constitutes sufficient evidence of transfer — how much, in what conditions, over what period — is undefined. This belongs jointly to a future Progress Evaluation model.

6. **Group composition change mid-progression (Medium).** Clubs re-form groups. What happens to a group's standing objectives and progression when its membership or purpose changes partway through a cycle is not addressed, and naive handling would either strand individual progressions or reset them unnecessarily.

7. **Player understanding is required but its mechanism is unspecified (Low/Medium).** Objectives should be owned by the player's understanding, yet Atlas communicates through the coach and never steps between coach and player. How the player's understanding is established and confirmed without violating that boundary is unresolved.

## Closing Statement

Training is where everything Atlas knows finally meets the table. Reasoning produces understanding, prioritization decides what matters, and training is where that decision becomes an athlete who is measurably better than they were.

Atlas designs training that serves the long arc: few objectives pursued properly, sequenced in the natural order of development, matched to what the player can absorb today, varied in method but consistent in purpose, honest about load and recovery, and judged by whether the skill survives into real play. It designs for groups without dissolving the individual, and for individuals without isolating them from the competitive context that makes skill real.

And it hands every design to the coach as a proposal — explained, bounded, adaptable, and open to being changed or refused. Atlas supplies the reasoning. The coach supplies the coaching.
